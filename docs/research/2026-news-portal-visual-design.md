# 2026 News Portal Visual Design Research

Research conducted to inform the redesign of News Triangulator's reading page (the generated cross-source "Article"), its homepage/listing page, and — as of Sections 8–10, added in a later pass — its masthead/header, footer, and admin login screen, plus — as of Section 11, added in a further later pass — its reusable UI component primitives (buttons, forms, badges, loading/empty states). Sources are primary where possible: newsroom design-system docs (Washington Post WPDS, BBC GEL, Financial Times Origami), type-foundry case studies (Klim Type Foundry on FT's Financier, Commercial Type on Guardian Egyptian), first-party redesign write-ups (ProPublica's own "Why We Redesigned" article, an OpenNews "Source" interview with NYT engineers/designers, itsnicethat's designer interview on the NYT Magazine redesign), and Wikipedia's and Ground News's own product documentation for citation/multi-source UI. A few generic trend-listicle searches turned up nothing worth citing and were discarded rather than padded in; those gaps are called out explicitly below. Headline finding: no major outlet frames its redesign around abstract "trends" — each concrete typographic or layout choice is justified by a specific operational problem (mobile share of traffic, page-load speed, ad-slot flexibility, cross-platform legibility), which is the same standard this document tries to hold claims to.

## 1. Typography

**NYT: role-based type system, not one headline font.** The Times runs distinct faces per role: NYT Cheltenham (a custom Matthew Carter reworking of the 1896 Cheltenham, commissioned by design director Tom Bodkin) for most headlines; NYT Stymie/Karnak for magazine and long-form display heads needing a "louder, heavier" presence; NYT Franklin for captions, metadata and navigation (replacing an older reliance on Arial and all-caps Georgia); Georgia for running body text; NYT Imperial as the serif body alternative. Lighter/extra-light Cheltenham weights are used for features to differentiate them from heavier weights on hard news, i.e. weight itself carries a section signal. (Source: [Fonts In Use — NYT Cheltenham](https://fontsinuse.com/typefaces/7802/nyt-cheltenham), [Fonts In Use — NYT article redesign, May 2013](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/3907/the-new-york-times-article-redesign-may-2013))

**NYT: the 2013 redesign was about consistency and flexibility, not a font swap.** In an OpenNews "Source" interview, NYT engineer Eitan Konigsburg and designers described eliminating pagination (longer, continuous pages) and the difficulty of "evenly dispersing text and image content" without elements crowding on narrow screens; the team had to support 126 layout variants across content type, viewport and ad configuration. Designer Allen Tan noted the custom type system's job was to let readers "distinguish 'hard news' headlines from lighter, feature-y pieces, from opinion pieces" at a glance — typography as a wayfinding signal, not just a headline treatment. **Actionable takeaway:** if the Article page needs to visually distinguish generated narrative from quoted/attributed material, a weight or role distinction (not just color) is a validated pattern. (Source: [Source (OpenNews) — Behind the Scenes on the NYT Redesign](https://source.opennews.org/articles/behind-scenes-nyt-redesign/))

**NYT Magazine (2020s): reimagining an old face for new platforms, keeping the brand's "spirit."** Type designer Henrik Kubel (A2-Type) reworked the Magazine's Cheltenham-derived display face and a Franklin Gothic revival for a "modern, multi-platform" edit, with specific attention to a more literary italic and dedicated emphasis characters. Design director Gail Bichler's stated rationale: "when there's value in a brand, it's important to retain the essence of what it is... there's a certain spirit to the work that should remain intact" — evolution, not replacement. (Source: [It's Nice That — interview with Gail Bichler on the NYT Magazine redesign](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/gail-bichler-the-new-york-times-magazine-redesign-publication-spotlight-080426))

**FT: commissioned a bespoke serif (Financier) specifically to survive the shift to a narrower, more spacious digital-first grid.** For its 2014 redesign the FT moved from an 8-column broadsheet grid toward a 6-column layout and needed one serif family that read as authoritative across finance, arts, science and sport, and worked from print broadsheet down to mobile. Kris Sowersby (Klim Type Foundry) designed Financier with no ink traps — instead deep, sharp joins and beaked terminals to resist ink spread on newsprint — and split it into a Display cut (headlines, inspired by Perpetua's inscriptional quality) and a Text cut (body, drawing on Solus's efficiency), deliberately "visually compatible rather than consistent" so the two feel related without being identical. Tall ascenders were chosen specifically because they hold up in vertical-scroll digital reading. **Actionable takeaway:** a headline/body serif pairing from the same family (rather than two unrelated typefaces) is the FT's answer to needing both authority in a hero headline and comfort in a long article body. (Source: [Klim Type Foundry — Financier design information](https://klim.co.nz/blog/financier-design-information/))

**Guardian: chose a slab serif specifically to look unlike other papers.** For its 2018 format redesign, the Guardian commissioned Commercial Type (Christian Schwartz and Paul Barnes, art directed by Mark Porter) to build Guardian Egyptian Headline. The brief called for a serif, and the studio deliberately picked a low-contrast Egyptian/slab construction over "a more traditional news headline face" specifically to differentiate the paper's voice from competitors; the full family also includes a Text Egyptian for body and a four-width Sans for headline flexibility. This was a full "typographic voice" project, not a single-font swap. (Source: [Commercial Type — search result summary of the Guardian Egyptian showcase](https://commercialtype.com/catalog/guardian_egyptian); direct fetch of the showcase page failed with a connection error, so this claim rests on the foundry's own catalog copy surfaced via search, not a directly-read case-study page — flagged as thinner-than-ideal sourcing)

**BBC: legibility is the explicit top priority, stated as policy, not preference.** BBC's GEL (Global Experience Language) typography guide states its philosophy plainly: "inclusive content must be readable, and for it to be readable it must first be legible." Concretely: body text 15–18px depending on viewport, set in relative units (em/rem) so OS/browser font-size preferences are honored; measure capped at 80 characters with 60–70 as the target, controlled via `ch` units and `max-width` so text reflows correctly under user font-size changes; headings get tighter line-height (1.125) while body copy (set in Reith Serif) uses 1.375 for comfortable long-form reading. (Source: [BBC GEL — Typography foundations](https://bbc.github.io/gel/foundations/typography/))

**Serif vs. sans conclusion for this project:** every long-form-focused outlet examined (NYT, FT, Guardian, BBC) uses a serif for body copy and/or headlines in its primary reading experience, and each justifies it functionally (authority, differentiation, or legibility) rather than by trend. None of them uses a pure geometric sans as the reading face for long-form body text. For an "Article" page whose whole premise is trustworthy synthesis, a serif body face in the FT/BBC mold (comfortable line-height, capped measure) is the better-evidenced choice than a generic app sans.

## 2. Color Palettes and Visual Tone

Primary-source coverage here is genuinely thin — most outlets' color rationale lives inside proprietary brand guidelines that aren't public, and generic "2025/2026 color trend" listicles (Pantone-of-the-year roundups, etc.) are exactly the secondary-aggregator content this research was told to avoid, so none of that was cited.

**Washington Post (WPDS): a large named palette organized by semantic role, not raw hex values.** WPDS's public design-system docs organize color into named hue families (red, spark, orange, mustard, gold, yellow, green, teal, blue, purple, pink, gray), each with numbered steps (0–700), and layer semantic tokens on top: primary/secondary with hover states, "on-color" tokens for text/icon/stroke placed over a given surface, and dedicated error/success/warning/signal containers. The system explicitly separates "theme" (app-level color choices) from "mode" (system light/dark setting) as different axes. Accessibility target is AAA (7:1 contrast), stricter than WCAG's AA minimums. **Actionable takeaway:** don't pick a handful of hex values ad hoc — define a small set of semantic roles (agreement / contradiction / omission / framing, given this project's four-dimension breakdown) as tokens, each with its own light- and dark-mode value, rather than hardcoding colors per component. (Source: [WPDS — Color foundations](https://build.washingtonpost.com/foundations/color))

**FT: a restrained, near-monochrome core with one or two brand accents, not a broad palette.** FT's Origami `o-colors` module frames it as a small palette of named colors (teal, oxford, claret, etc.) exposed as CSS custom properties, with FT's actual brand pink and grey called out as having only "a limited number of valid use-cases for digital UI" — i.e. the brand color is deliberately used sparingly, not as a dominant UI color. (Source: [npm — @financial-times/o-colors package description](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@financial-times/o-colors); GitHub README itself did not render on fetch, so this rests on the package registry's mirrored description rather than the full README — flagged as thinner sourcing)

**Verge (Vox Media, 2022 rebrand): bright/dark as a deliberate stance, not a fallback.** The Verge's 2022 redesign launched with the homepage dark-only (white/grey text on black, with saturated accent colors), treating dark mode as the primary brand expression rather than a secondary preference; a light-mode toggle was explicitly a later roadmap item, not day-one. This is the one clear example found of an outlet treating "cool/tech, high-saturation-accent" tone as an intentional identity choice rather than an editorial-warmth one. (Source: search-derived summary citing TypeRoom/Fonts In Use coverage of the 2022 Verge rebrand — not independently re-fetched from a Vox Media first-party post, since Vox did not appear to have a public design blog post on it; flagged as secondary-sourced)

**Conclusion:** the strongest, best-evidenced pattern is WPDS's — a constrained, semantically-named palette with light/dark pairs baked in per token — rather than a single "warm editorial" or "cool tech" hex sheet. Given this project's four-dimension breakdown (agreement/contradiction/omission/framing), that token-per-role approach maps directly onto product needs and is preferable to picking a generic "warm editorial" palette off a trend list.

## 3. Layout Density and Whitespace for Long-Form Reading

**BBC's measure guidance is the most concrete, load-bearing number found in this research:** cap line length at 80 characters, target 60–70, implemented with `ch` units and `max-width` (not fixed pixel widths) so it survives user font-size changes. (Source: [BBC GEL — Typography](https://bbc.github.io/gel/foundations/typography/))

**NYT's 2013 shift away from pagination is directly relevant to a synthesized long article.** The stated problem was less about aesthetics than about "evenly dispersing text and image content" so blocks didn't crowd on narrow viewports — i.e. whitespace/density decisions were driven by the need to support dozens of layout variants (content type × viewport × ad placement), not a single fixed template. For a page that must render a variable-length generated narrative plus inline citations, this argues for a flexible content-flow system over a rigid fixed-height template. (Source: [Source (OpenNews) — Behind the Scenes on the NYT Redesign](https://source.opennews.org/articles/behind-scenes-nyt-redesign/))

**BBC's line-height split (headline 1.125 vs. body 1.375)** is a concrete, transferable number for distinguishing dense headline blocks from an airier reading body. (Source: [BBC GEL — Typography](https://bbc.github.io/gel/foundations/typography/))

No outlet's public material discusses whitespace/density in the abstract ("more white space is a 2026 trend") — every concrete number found was tied to a measure or line-height rule, which is why this section is shorter than a listicle would make it; that's a deliberate reflection of what primary sources actually said.

## 4. Multi-Source Attribution / Citation Presentation — most directly relevant topic

**Wikipedia: superscript marker + hover popup + backlinked reference list is the most mature, battle-tested pattern for exactly this problem.** Concretely, per Wikipedia's own Help:Footnotes documentation: inline citation markers render as a bracketed, superscripted number/letter (`[1]`, `[a]`); with JavaScript enabled, hovering the marker on desktop pops up a preview box showing the citation content without navigating away; clicking jumps to and highlights the matching entry in the reference list; each reference-list entry carries a caret (`^`) backlink for single-use citations, or lettered backlinks (`a`, `b`, `c`, ...) when the same citation is reused at multiple points in the body; reference lists with 10+ entries auto-flow into responsive columns. **This maps almost exactly onto News Triangulator's stated need for "inline hover-citations back to each source":** superscript-marker-plus-hover-preview is a proven, low-friction way to keep prose readable while making every claim traceable, and the reused-citation backlink pattern (a/b/c) is directly useful if the same source is cited multiple times across one generated Article. (Source: [Wikipedia — Help:Footnotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Footnotes))

**Ground News: merges same-story coverage and layers bias/reliability metadata directly onto the merged story, rather than just linking out.** Per Ground News's own about page, articles covering the same event are algorithmically merged into a single story page; that page then displays a "Bias Bar" (visual left/center/right distribution of the outlets covering it), a factuality/reliability score, and ownership data for each source, all as context layered onto the merged story rather than as a plain list of links. The "Blindspot" feature specifically surfaces stories that are heavily covered on one side of the spectrum and nearly absent on the other, framed as countering algorithmic echo chambers. **Actionable takeaway directly applicable here:** Ground News's core move — merge coverage into one entity, then attach a compact visual strip of per-source metadata to the top of the merged story — is structurally the closest existing analog to News Triangulator's own agreement/contradiction/omission breakdown, and validates putting a compact "which sources, what leaning/reliability" strip near the top of a generated Article rather than only inline. (Source: [Ground News — About page](https://ground.news/about))

**AllSides was investigated but could not be verified first-hand:** every direct fetch of allsides.com (the FAQ, the media bias chart blog post, the general news page) returned HTTP 403 (the site appears to block automated fetching). What's known about its left/center/right column layout and its per-story "read the other side" comparison comes only from secondary description in search results, not a directly-read primary page, so per this research's own sourcing rule it is not cited as a verified claim here. If AllSides's UI specifically matters for the redesign, it needs a manual (human-browser) look rather than automated fetch.

**Genius-style span-highlight annotations were investigated but yielded only shallow, mostly-secondary coverage** (a 2015 TechCrunch piece on Genius's web-annotation expansion, plus generic implementation how-tos). The interaction model — select/highlight a span of text, attach an expandable annotation, let readers open it without leaving the page — is directionally relevant to inline citations but no first-party Genius design writeup was found, so this is flagged as a thin, not a well-evidenced, source of the four requested for this topic.

**Overall for topic 4:** the two well-evidenced patterns — Wikipedia's superscript-hover-backlink system, and Ground News's merged-story-plus-metadata-strip — are complementary rather than competing, and both point the same direction: inline superscript/hover for per-claim traceability, plus a compact per-source summary strip near the top of the page for at-a-glance source composition.

## 5. Dark Mode

**WPDS treats dark mode as a first-class, systemic design axis, not an inverted stylesheet.** Colors are defined via semantic tokens (surface, surface-highest, "portal," "on-*" text/icon tokens) whose values change per mode to preserve visual hierarchy and elevation, rather than the UI simply inverting light values. WPDS explicitly distinguishes "theme" (a product/brand-level color choice) from "mode" (the system-wide light/dark setting), meaning a given theme still needs distinct light and dark value sets. Baseline dark background/surface is a dark gray (documented as `gray700`), not pure black. Accessibility target is AAA/7:1 contrast in both modes. (Source: [WPDS — Color foundations](https://build.washingtonpost.com/foundations/color))

**ProPublica's 2026 redesign shipped a light/dark/auto toggle** ("Change Appearance: Auto / Light / Dark") as part of its cross-platform visual refresh, confirming dark-mode support is now baseline expectation for a serious news product, though ProPublica's own article gives no further detail on the specific values used. (Source: [ProPublica — Why We Redesigned](https://www.propublica.org/article/why-propublica-redesign))

**The Verge's 2022 relaunch went further and made dark the default/primary experience** (not an opt-in), with a light-mode toggle arriving later as a roadmap item — evidence that at least one major outlet treats dark as the primary brand surface rather than an accessibility add-on. (Source: search-derived, not independently re-fetched — see caveat in Section 2)

**Actionable takeaway:** don't design a light theme and auto-invert it. Follow WPDS's model — define semantic surface/text/border tokens, give each an explicit light and dark value (with dark using a dark gray rather than true black for the base surface, consistent with reducing eye strain per general accessibility guidance), and keep contrast ratios at least at WCAG AA and ideally AAA for body text given this is a reading-heavy page.

## 6. Mobile-First / Responsive Patterns for Long Article Reading

**The Guardian's 2025 relaunch is the most concrete mobile-first case study found, though sourced from a design trade publication rather than the Guardian's own blog.** Per Creative Boom's coverage, the redesign was driven by the fact 75% of the Guardian's audience is on mobile, and is described as "a ground-up rethink of how news should be consumed on your phone" rather than a responsive adaptation of a desktop layout. Concrete changes: flexible image handling that no longer forces landscape-only photos (i.e. the layout adapts to portrait/vertical imagery rather than cropping it to fit a desktop-shaped slot), native support for publishing without a hero image when nothing suitable exists, and vertical video support built into the layout grid. Sections gained the ability to carry their own typographic/layout identity rather than sharing one rigid template — explicitly framed as a reaction against most news sites currently "looking the same." Note: this article contains no direct designer quotes and is not itself a first-party Guardian source, so it should be treated as reporting-about rather than the Guardian's own account. (Source: [Creative Boom — The Guardian's 2025 redesign](https://www.creativeboom.com/news/the-guardians-2025-redesign-is-the-upgrade-its-readers-deserve/))

**BBC News was historically an early mover on mobile-first responsive news.** BBC designer Mark Hurrell's own portfolio writeup states the News site redesign became "one of the first major mobile-first responsive websites" as mobile traffic grew, built via prototyped responsive versions that fed into what became the BBC GEL design system; the redesign was validated through "hypothesis driven, interactive prototypes" tested in lab research across primary markets, and reportedly drove a 50% increase in pageviews shortly after launch. (Source: [Mark Hurrell — BBC News website redesign](https://markhurrell.com/work/bbc-news-redesign/))

**BBC's `ch`-unit/max-width measure rule (Section 3) is itself a mobile-relevant pattern**, since it's designed to keep line length in the 60–80 character range across viewport sizes rather than defining separate fixed-width rules per breakpoint. (Source: [BBC GEL — Typography](https://bbc.github.io/gel/foundations/typography/))

**Actionable takeaway:** treat mobile not as a breakpoint of a desktop Article layout but as the primary target — this matters specifically for how inline hover-citations are designed, since "hover" has no direct mobile equivalent and will need a tap-to-reveal or tap-and-hold fallback (Wikipedia's own mobile citation handling — tap to jump to the footnote rather than hover-preview — is consistent with this, per the same Help:Footnotes page, though the page's detail on mobile-specific behavior is thinner than its desktop hover description).

## 7. Homepage / Listing-Page Layout Conventions

**BBC GEL's "volumes" concept: homepage as a set of independently swappable modules, not a fixed grid.** Per Mark Hurrell's account, the BBC News homepage used multiple configurable layout modules ("volumes") that let editors "adapt the page constantly through the days to reflect differing combinations of editorial priorities and available imagery" — i.e. the homepage's job is treated as an editorially-driven composition problem, not a fixed template with slots. (Source: [Mark Hurrell — BBC News website redesign](https://markhurrell.com/work/bbc-news-redesign/))

**ProPublica's 2026 homepage redesign bundles investigation + context, and resurfaces archive material.** Per ProPublica's own article, investigations are now packaged on the homepage/listing level with their supporting material (visual explainers, methodology notes, tip-submission routes) bundled together rather than scattered, "so it's easier for you to find the full picture," and the homepage now deliberately surfaces past investigations from the archive (e.g. Pulitzer-finalist 2025 reporting) rather than showing only the most recent items. Journalist/partner bylines now carry photos and secure-contact info directly in the listing-adjacent material. **Actionable takeaway directly relevant to this project:** a listing page for generated cross-source Articles could similarly surface each Article's "supporting material" (the four-dimension breakdown, source list) as part of the card/listing entry rather than requiring a click-through, and resurface older but still-relevant Articles rather than a purely reverse-chronological feed. (Source: [ProPublica — Why We Redesigned](https://www.propublica.org/article/why-propublica-redesign))

**NYT's 2013–2018-era homepage moved toward loosely-hierarchical topical blocks rather than one strict ranked list**, with different block types (breaking news with bullet summaries, feature blocks with pull quotes) customized per section rather than one card template reused everywhere — though this detail comes from secondary description of the redesign rather than a directly-fetched NYT Open post (no live nytimes.com/open post on this specific topic could be located), so it's flagged as thinner sourcing. (Source: search-derived summary; not independently re-fetched from a primary NYT post)

**The Verge's 2022 relaunch turned its homepage into a social-feed-like stream** mixing original reporting with external conversation/social commentary, a deliberate departure from a traditional ranked-headline homepage — again sourced secondarily (TypeRoom/media coverage), since no first-party Vox Media design post was located. (Source: search-derived, see caveat in Section 2)

**Axios's card-based "Smart Brevity" format is a relevant density pattern for a listing page even though it's not a traditional newspaper homepage.** Per Axios's own Smart Brevity page, content is built as discrete cards (a typical newsletter edition runs 6–10), each leading with "what's new" and "why it matters" up front, using bold text, bullets and white space so a reader can stop at any card or skim past it — validated through eye-tracking research and reported to cut average read time ~40%. **Actionable takeaway:** a homepage listing of generated Articles could borrow this card structure — a bolded one-line "what's agreed / what's disputed" lede per card — to let readers judge relevance before clicking into the full four-dimension breakdown. (Source: [Axios — Smart Brevity](https://www.axios.com/smart-brevity))

## 8. Masthead / Header & Nameplate Typography

**Consumer newspapers: the nameplate is treated as a hand-drawn brand mark, not text set in a typeface.** For the New York Times, the masthead lettering is a proprietary, hand-drawn design rooted in Textura/Gothic blackletter dating to the paper's 1851 founding, most recently refined by type designer Ed Benguiat in 1967; the paper reportedly considered and rejected a cleaner sans-serif treatment in the 1960s, keeping the ornate blackletter specifically because it now functions as brand differentiation. For the Washington Post, the nameplate is likewise a custom blackletter/Gothic-style mark rather than a font-family instance — it is "hand drawn as a logo" and not implemented as a reusable typeface, distinct from the Post's own headline face (Postoni) and body type, which are separately documented on Type Network but explicitly do not cover the masthead itself. (Source: [Boing Boing — Chomsky, a blackletter typeface inspired by the NYT nameplate](https://boingboing.net/2019/12/19/chomsky-a-blackletter-typefac.html); [Cool Hunting — Advancing the Ancient Lettering of the New York Times Nameplate](https://coolhunting.com/design/history-of-the-new-york-times-nameplate/); [Type Network — Washington Post: Evolving Typography](https://typenetwork.com/articles/washington-post))

**The person behind many of these nameplates was a single lettering artist, and his own stated philosophy was explicitly anti-typeface.** Jim Parkinson (1941–2025) hand-lettered or restored newspaper nameplates for the Los Angeles Times, San Francisco Chronicle, Detroit Free Press, Chicago Tribune, Montreal Gazette, Wall Street Journal and others, alongside magazine logos (Rolling Stone, Esquire). Per a Society for News Design tribute quoting people who worked with him directly, Parkinson's guidance was that a nameplate should "breathe the DNA of the place" — treating each paper's mark as a one-off expression of that specific publication's identity, not an application of a shared system. Design journalist Mario Garcia described his restoration work as giving old blackletter nameplates "a bath," with "the typography emerging fresh and true to its original form," i.e. even his "modernizations" were framed as recovering an original design intent rather than replacing it. Parkinson himself said working on a publication's custom lettering was "much more fulfilling than all the other lettering work" he did. (Source: [Society for News Design — A tribute to the talented, prolific Jim Parkinson](https://snd.org/a-tribute-to-the-talented-prolific-jim-parkinson/); [Type Network — Lettering, logos, fonts: Jim Parkinson did them all](https://typenetwork.com/articles/lettering-logos-fonts-jim-parkinson-did-them-all))

**Wire services/agencies genuinely break this pattern — their marks read as standard-typeface wordmarks, not bespoke lettering, and this research could not verify why.** Reuters' current mark (designed by Interbrand in 2008) is documented on Fonts In Use as a wordmark set in Neo Sans, a commercially available geometric sans, not a custom letterform — a sharp contrast to NYT/WaPo/LA Times blackletter. Reuters' own logo history reinforces this: its 1965 identity (Alan Fletcher's 87-dot grid rendering of the word "Reuters") was itself an abstracted, systems-driven mark rather than lettering with any historical/calligraphic reference, and the 1996 replacement was a plain solidified wordmark. One design-commentary source frames Reuters' mark as functioning "more like an ingredient brand than a masthead" — appearing small and repeatedly in bylines across thousands of partner sites rather than as a single dominant page-top identity the way a consumer paper's nameplate does. **This research could not independently verify a first-party design rationale for why Reuters (or AP, AFP, Bloomberg) uses standard/geometric sans wordmarks instead of bespoke lettering** — no foundry case study, style guide, or first-party "how we built our masthead" post was found for any of the four wire services; the inference that this reflects a wire service's role as an unbylined, distributed, cross-syndicated content supplier (where the logo needs to work small and repeated, not dominate a front page the reader lingers on) is this document's own reasoning from the evidence, not a claim any primary source states directly. (Source: [Fonts In Use — Thomson Reuters logo](https://fontsinuse.com/uses/16532/thomson-reuters-logo); logo-history claims triangulated against non-primary logo-history aggregators, flagged below)

**Wire-service homepages themselves could not be directly observed — every one of reuters.com, apnews.com, bloomberg.com and afp.com blocked automated fetch**, so this document cannot describe their actual header structure (utility bar presence, nav density) first-hand; see Gaps below. What is verifiable is the BBC's own documented masthead pattern, which is architecturally the closest publicly-documented analog to a wire-style utility-first header even though the BBC is a broadcaster, not a wire agency: BBC's GEL design system specifies the Masthead as two stacked parts — a **global navigation band** (BBC logo, account controls, notifications, a small set of promoted cross-site links like News/Sport/Weather/iPlayer, a "More" overflow menu, and search) sitting above a **local site banner** (that site's own logo plus its homepage link). The global band is deliberately minimal and utility-first — it is not the place editorial identity lives — while each local site's banner is where that site's specific brand identity is expressed. (Source: [BBC GEL — Masthead component](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/masthead/))

**Actionable takeaway:** the project's chosen "Wire Service" identity is closer to the Reuters/wire pattern than the NYT/WaPo pattern, and the two are genuinely different traditions, not two points on the same spectrum — consumer papers spend their masthead as a standalone brand statement (bespoke lettering, "DNA of the place"), while what evidence exists suggests wire marks are built to recur small and often rather than to dominate. A centered serif *nameplate* set in an actual typeface (not hand-lettered blackletter) is consistent with this project's existing choice and with the wire-service evidence found, but it should not be dressed up with NYT-style ornamentation, since that would borrow the wrong tradition. The BBC's two-band masthead structure (thin utility band above, brand identity band below) is a validated, directly-sourced pattern for exactly the "utility bar above, nameplate below, nav further below" structure already chosen in ticket 22 — it is not wire-specific, but it is the only first-party documented masthead *structure* (as opposed to logo design) found in this research.

## 9. Footer Conventions

**Direct observation of ProPublica's live site (fetched first-hand) shows a footer that is deliberately denser and typographically quieter than the header, organized as a sitemap rather than a restatement of primary navigation.** ProPublica's header is a minimal masthead (text nameplate, tagline, small utility links for donate/search, and a short primary nav for About/Newsletters/Podcast/Tips). Its footer, by contrast, is organized into five distinct columns — Info (About, Impact, Leadership, Staff, Partners, Awards, Events, Jobs, Fellowships), Coverage (topic links), Local (regional reporting sections), Contact (General Inquiries, Tips, Corrections, Freelance Pitches, Media Inquiries), and Support (donation methods) — set in a smaller sans-serif face against the header's larger, bolder display type. The footer's job here is clearly information-architecture (a near-complete sitemap plus every institutional/contact channel a reader or source might need), not brand expression. (Source: direct observation of [propublica.org](https://www.propublica.org/), fetched 2026-08-17)

**None of the three design systems already used as primary sources elsewhere in this document (WPDS, BBC GEL, FT Origami) publish a documented footer *pattern* — this is a genuine, notable gap rather than an oversight in this research.** WPDS's own docs site (build.washingtonpost.com) was checked directly: it documents Foundations (color, type, spacing, icons, logos) and 23+ discrete UI components (accordion, app bar, dialog, drawer, navigation menu, etc.), but has no dedicated "Footer" or "Masthead" entry — the live washingtonpost.com footer exists, but it is evidently treated as page-level editorial assembly rather than a documented, reusable design-system component. BBC GEL documents a Masthead component in detail (see Section 8) but no equivalent Footer component could be located in its component library. FT's Origami system does register `o-footer` ("FT branded page footer for responsive pages") as a discrete component, confirming FT treats the footer as a first-class reusable module the way it does the header (`o-header`) — but the repository was inaccessible to this research (GitHub returned 403/404 on the README on every path tried), so nothing about its actual visual content could be verified beyond the component's existence and stated purpose. **Actionable takeaway:** treat the footer, like ProPublica's, as an information-architecture surface (sitemap, contact/tip channels, institutional links) styled in smaller, quieter utility type than the header/nameplate — this is the one concrete pattern this research could verify first-hand — but do not expect to find an authoritative "footer design spec" the way one exists for typography or color; none of the outlets examined appears to publicly formalize one.

## 10. Login / Auth-Gate Design for an Editorial Product

**This section's sourcing is genuinely thin, exactly as anticipated — flagged honestly rather than padded.** No first-party design write-up (an NYT Open post, a WaPo engineering-blog post, a Guardian internal design post) describing the *visual* design of either a subscriber paywall gate or an internal newsroom login tool could be located. What follows is assembled from adjacent, partial evidence.

**(a) Public subscriber gates: the available evidence is about the *mechanism*, not the visual tone, of the gate.** The best-documented example is the New York Times's "Dynamic Meter" — a machine-learning-driven metered paywall (in place since 2011, retuned in 2019) that decides per-reader when to show a registration/subscription wall, optimizing a conversion-vs-engagement trade-off via causal ML rather than a fixed article count. This is a substantive, verifiable claim about *how* NYT decides when to gate a reader, but it comes from secondary tech-press coverage (VentureBeat), not a directly-fetched NYT Open engineering post, and it says nothing about the gate's visual design. Direct fetches of nytimes.com, theguardian.com and washingtonpost.com's subscription/login pages were all blocked (see Gaps), so this document cannot describe their actual paywall-screen layouts first-hand. What is independently verifiable is that most large publishers now run their paywall through a third-party subscription-commerce platform (Piano, or Zephr — now part of Zuora) rather than a fully bespoke in-house gate: Zephr's own product marketing describes "50+ pre-built decision nodes" for building "personalized subscriber journeys" and dynamically showing/hiding/customizing paywalls per reader. This confirms that for major outlets the paywall is commonly a *configured instance of a vendor product*, which is one plausible reason a distinct, publisher-specific "paywall design language" is hard to find documented anywhere — it may not be a bespoke design decision at most outlets in the first place. (Source: [VentureBeat — How machine learning helps the New York Times power its paywall](https://venturebeat.com/ai/how-machine-learning-helps-the-new-york-times-power-its-paywall); [Zuora — Zephr AI Paywalls for Media & Digital Publishers](https://www.zuora.com/products/zephr/))

**(b) Internal/back-office editorial tooling: primary sourcing is essentially absent, as expected for internal tooling — the one substantive, well-documented example found is adjacent rather than exact.** The Washington Post's Arc XP (originally an in-house CMS built inside the Post's own newsroom starting 2011, later commercialized) is the closest thing to a publicly-known "how a wire-style newsroom's internal tool is built" story, and its marketing materials name its internal modules (Composer for headless authoring, Web Sked for scheduling, Page Builder for page assembly) — but no screenshot, design-system doc, or design write-up describing *how those interfaces look or feel* could be fetched; arcxp.com's own product pages returned only navigation/marketing copy, not descriptive or visual content, on direct fetch. The one concretely-sourced data point about a professional, non-consumer, editorial-adjacent tool deliberately resisting polished/marketing-style design is the **Bloomberg Terminal** — not a CMS, but a financial data terminal used by (among others) Bloomberg's own newsroom, and worth citing cautiously with that caveat. Per UX Magazine's design-trade analysis, Bloomberg has repeatedly resisted consumer-style redesigns of the Terminal's famously dense, black-background, yellow-on-black interface; a Bloomberg executive is quoted insisting the company must be "religiously consistent" because users have come to read the terminal's dated, complex look as a professional status marker ("you can see a Bloomberg from a mile away"), and the article's own framing is that "the more painful the UI is, the more satisfied these users are." That is a real, sourced example of a professional tool's design language being deliberately *anti*-consumer-polish — but it describes a trading terminal's internal culture, not an editorial CMS's, and should not be read as describing how AP, Reuters or AFP's actual internal newsroom systems look; no such description of any wire service's internal tooling was found anywhere in this research. (Source: [UX Magazine — The Impossible Bloomberg Makeover](https://uxmag.com/articles/the-impossible-bloomberg-makeover); [Arc XP — CMS for Newspapers and News Publishing](https://www.arcxp.com/solutions/news-publishing/))

**Actionable takeaway:** because this project's login is admin/reviewer-only rather than a public paywall, the relevant precedent is (b), not (a), and (b) is exactly the case with almost no verifiable primary design sourcing anywhere in the industry — which is itself informative. It supports treating the login as unglamorous back-office infrastructure (plain form, no marketing copy, no "unlock premium content" framing, no hero imagery) rather than reaching for a subscriber-paywall visual language that would be both unsourced *and* the wrong precedent for an internal reviewer tool. The Bloomberg Terminal evidence, read cautiously, at least supports one directional choice: a professional/internal tool earns trust through consistency and plainness, not through visual polish borrowed from the reader-facing product.

## Synthesis: Header, Footer and Login as One System

Taken together, Sections 8–10 point toward the same underlying distinction this project already made when it picked "Wire Service" over a consumer-newspaper identity: a wire-style masthead should be a *typeset* nameplate rather than bespoke blackletter lettering (Section 8), because the evidence suggests wire marks are built to recur small and often rather than to dominate a front page the way NYT's or WaPo's hand-lettered marks do. The footer, per the one first-hand example this research could verify (ProPublica) and the near-total absence of a documented "footer design system" anywhere else, should function as a dense, quietly-typeset sitemap/utility surface — structurally different in density and weight from the header's restrained nameplate, but drawn from the same type family and token set, the way ProPublica's smaller footer sans still reads as the same brand as its header (Section 9). The login screen, being an internal reviewer gate rather than a subscriber paywall, has the thinnest evidence base of the three (Section 10), but what little is verifiable argues against borrowing the public-paywall visual vocabulary (marketing copy, upsell framing) at all — it should look like the plainest, most utilitarian surface in the product, closer in spirit to the Bloomberg Terminal's unglamorous consistency than to any subscription gate. A coherent Wire Service identity, then, is not "the same decorative treatment applied everywhere" but the same restrained type system and utility-first structure applied at different densities depending on each surface's actual job: a quiet nameplate at the top, a dense sitemap at the bottom, and a bare, unbranded form for the one screen only staff will ever see.

## 11. UI Component Primitives

Research conducted in a further later pass, prompted by the observation that Sections 8–10's "Wire Service" identity work (masthead, footer, login) would feel inconsistent if every other reusable control in the product — buttons, form fields, status indicators, loading/empty states — stayed at default/generic styling. The same three design systems used elsewhere in this document (WPDS, BBC GEL, FT Origami) were checked for whether they document these specific primitives, using the same rule as the rest of the research: cite only what could actually be read, and say plainly when a system doesn't document something rather than filling the gap with generic UI-kit advice. One methodology note used repeatedly below: build.washingtonpost.com returns HTTP 200 with real content for a component slug that exists (verified against known-good pages like `/components/button` and `/components/card`) and a uniform HTTP 403 for a slug that doesn't (verified against several probes below) — that 403-vs-200 contrast is used as evidence of non-existence in this section, not just as a fetch failure.

### 11.1 Buttons

**WPDS documents a Button component with three variants and a fill/outline axis, not a large button taxonomy.** Per WPDS's own docs, Button ships "3 variants available `primary` `secondary` & `cta`," an `isOutline` prop that "will set the button appearance as an outline," and a `density` prop (`compact`/`default`) with the instruction that "buttons within the same layout should have the same density." Guidance favors text-plus-icon over icon-only ("when possible use text button with an icon... the pairing of text acts as a label to help contextualize the action being taken"), and sets a soft minimum width ("should still avoid being smaller than 120px"). No border-radius, weight, or color specifics are given at the component-doc level — those live in the token system (Section 2). The separately-documented Checkbox component reuses the identical `primary`/`secondary`/`cta` variant set plus a `fill`/`outline` style axis, suggesting this three-variant, fill-or-outline vocabulary is WPDS's house pattern across interactive controls generally, not a one-off for Button. (Source: [WPDS — Button](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/button), [WPDS — Checkbox](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/checkbox))

**BBC GEL treats "button" and "CTA" as visually distinct by design, and ties the distinction to semantics, not decoration.** GEL's Buttons and CTAs guide draws a hard line on markup first — "links should be used where navigation is elicited, and `<button>`s where a non-navigational action is being invoked," warning that "an element announced as a link that behaves as a button creates a confusing and off-putting experience" for assistive-technology users — and only then gets to visual treatment: "a common approach is to give all `<button>`s a background colour, but make CTAs 'ghosts' (with a transparent background but a surrounding border)," on the principle that "form should follow function." It requires shared, consistent styling within each category ("be consistent with colour, font size and weight, and padding") and strong, distinct hover/focus states ("a solid" outline, "optionally with an offset") rather than subtle ones. Icon-only buttons still require a visible or accessible text label. (Source: [BBC GEL — Buttons and CTAs](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/buttons-and-ctas/))

**FT's own button-styles discussion (found in a GitHub issue, not a rendered doc page) independently arrives at the same solid-vs-outline split, plus a named destructive variant.** `o-buttons`' README could not be rendered by the fetch tool (same failure pattern as other FT Origami repos elsewhere in this document — see Gaps), but engineering issue #34 on the same repo documents the actual style decisions in prose: "the solid blue style will stay as the standout but will be reserved for calls to action" (i.e. solid/filled color is spent on exactly one primary action type); an outline style in blue (default) or grey (a color-free variant) with an inverse scheme for dark backgrounds, serving as the secondary treatment; and an explicit destructive style — "solid red for negative/warning... same as blue but in red." A solid green is separately proposed for a positive/pressed state. This is thinner sourcing than WPDS's or BBC's live documentation (an engineering issue thread, not a published spec), but it is still first-party FT material, not a secondary aggregator. (Source: [GitHub — Financial-Times/o-buttons, issue #34 "New button styles"](https://github.com/Financial-Times/o-buttons/issues/34))

**The convergence across all three is the real finding here.** WPDS (solid `primary`/`cta` vs. `isOutline` secondary), BBC GEL (solid `<button>` vs. ghost/outline CTA), and FT (solid "standout," reserved for calls to action, vs. outline secondary, vs. a separately-colored destructive red) independently land on the same shape: **one saturated, filled treatment reserved for the single primary action in a given context; an outline/ghost treatment for everything secondary; and a distinct, separately-named color (red) reserved for destructive actions.** None of the three documents rounded-vs-square corner conventions, drop shadows, or gradient/skeuomorphic treatments — none of that generic SaaS button vocabulary appears in any of the three sources. **Actionable takeaway:** define exactly three button variants — primary (solid, one saturated brand/action color, used once per view for the actual next-step action), secondary (outline/ghost, same shape and padding as primary), and destructive (solid, a distinct red, reserved for irreversible actions like deleting a pending ingestion item) — and enforce BBC's button-vs-link semantic rule in markup (a `<button>` invokes an action; anything that navigates is an `<a>`), which is the one accessibility-relevant convention all three sources implicitly or explicitly share.

### 11.2 Forms and Inputs

**WPDS ships a large, granular family of form components rather than one generic "Input."** The full WPDS npm scope (checked directly against the npm registry, not search-engine snippets) includes `wpds-input-text`, `wpds-input-textarea`, `wpds-input-search`, `wpds-input-password`, `wpds-input-label`, `wpds-input-shared`, `wpds-error-message`, `wpds-helper-text`, `wpds-fieldset`, `wpds-checkbox`, `wpds-select`, `wpds-radio-group`, and `wpds-switch` as separate packages — confirming forms are treated as a first-class, decomposed part of the system, not an afterthought. Of the pages in that family this research could actually render (several, including `error-message`, `fieldset` and `helper-text`, returned the same 403 the Badge/Tag probes in 11.3 hit — inconsistent with their confirmed existence as packages, so likely a doc-site routing quirk rather than genuine absence; flagged as a partial gap), Input Text and Select both document the same pattern: a `label` prop required "for accessibility," a floating label that visibly shrinks once the field is focused or filled ("the label/placeholder is reduced in size" — Select), a boolean `required` prop paired with an undocumented "Show required" visual behavior, parallel `error`/`success` boolean props, an `errorMessage` string rendered "below the input/select to describe the cause," and a separate `helperText` string for non-error supplemental context. (Source: [WPDS — Input Text](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/input-text), [WPDS — Select](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/select))

**BBC GEL's Form Fields and Validation page is the single richest, most concretely actionable source found anywhere in this entire research document — including sections 1–10.** It is unambiguous, specific, and in several places counter-intuitive relative to common web convention, which is exactly the kind of primary-source specificity this research has tried to hold out for elsewhere. Concretely: labels sit **above** the field, never inside it as a placeholder-style overlay, because "a label over an input, similar to a placeholder, can misidentify the field as having a value" — and this matters most on mobile, where a virtual keyboard would otherwise obscure a side- or below-positioned label. On required-field marking, GEL explicitly reverses the usual asterisk convention: **"instead of indicating required fields, indicate optional ones. These should be fewer in number. Suffix the `<label>`'s text with '(optional)'."** `aria-required="true"` is added only once the user attempts to submit, not on initial page load. Validation is debounced as the user types, "giving the user a chance to type a full valid entry before an error message is displayed," with a general summary error appearing above the submit button on failed submission. Errors are wired with `aria-invalid` (toggled true/false live), `aria-describedby` linking the field to its error text, and a `role="alert"` container so the state change is announced to screen readers — and, critically, color is never the only signal: **"prefixing the error message with 'Error:', or a warning symbol, ensures the nature of the message is conveyed explicitly."** Placeholders are discouraged as a label substitute at all (cited reasons: unreliable accessible-name calculation, and problems for cognition, translation and interoperability); format hints instead go in a persistent `<small>` description. Native form elements are preferred over custom-built ones, and — another reversal of common practice — **submit buttons should never be disabled based on form validity**, since a disabled button with no explanation confuses users about why they can't proceed. (Source: [BBC GEL — Form fields and validation](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/form-fields/))

**FT's `o-forms` exists but is unreadable, the same access pattern as other FT Origami repos in this document.** The repo is confirmed to exist ("FT branded form elements"), was archived November 18, 2021 with work said to continue in "the Origami Component System," but its README did not render through the fetch tool and `raw.githubusercontent.com` returned 404 on both `main` and `master` branches — the identical failure signature already logged for `o-header`/`o-footer` in Section 9's gap notes. Nothing about FT's actual form-field visual conventions could be verified. (Source: [GitHub — Financial-Times/o-forms](https://github.com/Financial-Times/o-forms))

**Actionable takeaway:** adopt BBC's rule set directly for this project's forms (the ingestion-review screen, the admin login) — label above the field, mark the rare optional field rather than every required one, debounce validation rather than validating on every keystroke, prefix error text with an explicit "Error:" rather than relying on red text alone, wire `role="alert"`/`aria-invalid`/`aria-describedby`, and never disable the submit button. Use WPDS's parallel `error`/`success`/`helperText` prop shape (mapped onto the semantic color tokens this document's Section 2 already recommends) as the implementation-level structure for carrying that BBC-sourced behavior in code.

### 11.3 Badges / Status Pills / Tags

**Neither WPDS nor BBC GEL documents a badge, tag, chip, or status-pill component — and this was checked as rigorously as anything else in this research, not assumed.** A full-registry search of npm for every package under the `@washingtonpost` scope returned 52 packages; none is named `badge`, `tag`, `chip`, `pill`, `label` (in the status sense — `wpds-input-label` exists but is a form-field label, a different thing), or anything synonymous. Direct probes of `build.washingtonpost.com/components/{badge,tag,chip}` all returned the same 403 signature confirmed elsewhere to mean "no such slug" (contrasted against the 200s returned by `/components/button` and `/components/card`). On the BBC side, `bbc.github.io/gel/components/tags/` 404s outright, and the Cards component page — the one place a category/topic tag would most plausibly live, as part of a card's documented anatomy — lists only headline, content, description and toolbar; no tag, label or badge element appears anywhere in it. (Source: [npm registry search — `@washingtonpost/wpds`](https://registry.npmjs.org/-/v1/search?text=%40washingtonpost%2Fwpds), [WPDS — Card](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/card), [BBC GEL — Cards](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/cards/))

**The closest thing WPDS documents is Alert Banner, which is a page-level notification, not an inline status marker.** It ships four semantic variants — warning, information, success, error — described as "4 predefined alert banners each tied to our semantic messaging on our site," explicitly restricted to system status ("strictly for semantic messaging around system status") with a direct warning against repurposing it for anything promotional ("the use of including marketing or other messaging diminishes the effectiveness of semantic messaging"). It's dismissable, can carry a shadow "when overlaying content," and its anatomy is icon + text + a bar + a dismiss button — structurally a banner, not a small inline pill. (Source: [WPDS — Alert Banner](https://build.washingtonpost.com/components/alert-banner))

**FT's `o-labels` exists by name only — its content is as unverifiable as `o-header`/`o-footer`/`o-forms` elsewhere in this document.** The repository is real ("Standard labels styling") and was archived January 19, 2022, but the README did not render through the fetch tool, `raw.githubusercontent.com` 404s on both branch names tried, and `registry.origami.ft.com` — where the live component documentation is now said to live — could not be reached by the fetch tool at all. Its existence at least confirms FT treated inline labels/tags as a distinct, named, reusable component (unlike WPDS and BBC GEL, which apparently never formalized one), but nothing about its actual variants, colors or shape could be verified. (Source: [GitHub — Financial-Times/o-labels](https://github.com/Financial-Times/o-labels))

**Direct observation of ProPublica's live site (the same first-hand fetch method used successfully in Section 9) is the one genuinely conclusive data point here.** ProPublica uses topic tagging heavily and visibly — "Abortion," "Civil Rights," "Courts," "Criminal Justice," "Trump Administration," "DOGE" all appear as topic labels on article cards and in browse sections — but they render as **plain clickable text links**, not as colored rounded-pill or chip shapes. A real, actively-tagging newsroom, observed directly rather than inferred, does not dress its category tags up in badge chrome. (Source: direct observation of [propublica.org](https://www.propublica.org/), fetched 2026-08-17)

**This is the subsection where the instruction to be honest about generic-vs-newsroom content bites hardest, and the honest answer is: the colored rounded-pill badge is not a documented newsroom convention anywhere this research could verify.** It's a well-known generic SaaS/UI-kit pattern — every general-purpose component library (Bootstrap, Material, and dozens of others) documents one — but none of the three editorial design systems this document treats as primary sources publishes one, and the one live newsroom site checked first-hand uses plain links instead. **Actionable takeaway:** don't reach for a generic colored-pill badge component by default for this project's own status indicators (Draft/Pending/Complete on ingestion items, source-outlet tags on Article cards). If a status genuinely needs a compact visual marker rather than plain text, anchor its color vocabulary to WPDS's one documented editorial precedent for semantic status color — the four Alert Banner states (warning/information/success/error) — rather than an arbitrary palette, but keep the shape and weight plain and typographic (per BBC's overall restraint principle, Section 8's Synthesis) rather than defaulting to a saturated rounded pill purely because that's the generic-UI-kit default. Recognize explicitly that this project would be *inventing* this component, not following a found newsroom pattern — which is fine, but shouldn't be dressed up as "the wire-service way of doing it," because no evidence supports that framing.

### 11.4 Loading and Empty States

**WPDS documents no spinner, skeleton, progress, or loading-indicator component at all — confirmed the same way as 11.3's badge finding, not assumed.** The same 52-package npm-registry search that turned up zero badge/tag packages also turned up zero packages named `spinner`, `skeleton`, `loading`, or `progress`; direct probes of `build.washingtonpost.com/components/{spinner,skeleton,progress}` all returned the confirmed-absent 403 signature. Whatever loading treatment washingtonpost.com actually uses in production, it is not a documented, reusable WPDS component.

**BBC GEL's Load More component is the one substantively documented, first-party loading pattern found anywhere in this research, and it is specifically an editorial/content-list pattern, not a generic spinner demo.** On press, a spinner — "the standard Gel Iconography SVG icon, `gel-icon-loading`," rotating 360 degrees over a 1-second loop — appears directly above the button itself and is constrained to "not appear wider than the button," rather than taking over the page; a screen-reader-only live region announces "loading, please wait." The design deliberately rejects silent infinite scroll in favor of a manual, user-triggered "load more" action, citing infinite scroll's own known problems: "unsolicited scrolling resulting in misplacement" and "lack of clear feedback and focus management." After new content loads, a separator element announces the batch just added (e.g. "items 12 to 18") and receives keyboard focus so the reader lands somewhere sensible. GEL's separate Form Fields page adds one general, reusable rule for any loading indicator, in or out of this specific component: pair it with a textual label ("Loading" / "Please wait") that can be visually hidden but must remain available to screen readers. (Source: [BBC GEL — Load more](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/load-more/), [BBC GEL — Form fields and validation](https://bbc.github.io/gel/components/form-fields/))

**Neither WPDS nor BBC GEL documents an empty-state pattern (no results, nothing to show) anywhere this research could find — a genuine gap, stated plainly rather than papered over.** The Load More page's scope is explicitly "there is more content to fetch"; it says nothing about what to show when there's nothing at all. No other page in either system's documentation addresses an empty-results or zero-items case.

**FT's `o-loading` exists by name only, matching the exact access pattern already logged for `o-labels`, `o-forms`, `o-header` and `o-footer`.** The repo is real ("A visual loading indicator"), archived November 18, 2021, but the README did not render, raw.githubusercontent.com 404s, and the registry is unreachable — so, again, existence is the only verifiable fact. (Source: [GitHub — Financial-Times/o-loading](https://github.com/Financial-Times/o-loading))

**Actionable takeaway:** BBC's Load More pattern is directly reusable for this project's own paginated lists (a source list within an Article, a history/listing page of past Articles) — a small spinner scoped to the control being loaded rather than a full-page overlay, a screen-reader-only "loading" label, and a manual load-more affordance rather than silent infinite scroll. For empty states specifically (no pending ingestion items, no results for a search) there is no newsroom precedent anywhere in this research to draw on; that has to be original, restrained design work in the spirit of Section 10's login-screen conclusion (plain, unglamorous, no illustration/marketing copy) rather than something borrowed from a cited source — and it should be presented internally as exactly that: an original decision, not a researched one.

### Overall for topic 11

Buttons and forms are the two primitives with real, convergent, cross-system evidence behind them — enough to write concrete rules from (Sections 11.1–11.2). Badges/tags and loading/empty states are the two where the evidence runs out fast: no editorial design system examined documents a badge component, and only one (BBC GEL, via Load More) documents anything about loading state, with empty states undocumented everywhere. That asymmetry is itself the most important finding of this section — it means roughly half of this project's "make every control feel like the same wire-service product" goal can be grounded in a found, citable pattern, and the other half has to be designed from this project's own restraint principle (plain, typographic, unglamorous — the throughline of Sections 8–10's Synthesis) rather than from a newsroom precedent that doesn't exist in the public record.
